# Writing system: matthewgann.com

This is not a rule list. It is a priority hierarchy, a drafting method, and a set of diagnostics used
after the writing exists.

The previous version was a large collection of stylistic rules. It produced prose that passed the
checks and still read as generated, because it was fitted to the visible symptoms of AI writing
rather than to the causes. Case history is in `drafts/case-history.md`, kept as evidence rather than
as instruction.

**The goal is not prose that avoids AI tells. It is prose in which the thought is specific enough,
connected enough, and recognizably Matthew's that the tells mostly never arise.**

---

## 1. The hierarchy

When instructions conflict, resolve in this order. **A lower priority may never damage a higher
one.**

| | |
|---|---|
| 1 | Truth and provenance |
| 2 | Meaning and referential clarity |
| 3 | Logical and emotional continuity |
| 4 | The author's actual stance toward the reader |
| 5 | Integrity of the post type |
| 6 | Natural prose and rhythm |
| 7 | Venue conventions |
| 8 | Statistical style diagnostics |

Consequences, stated plainly because each of these has actually happened:

- Do not lengthen a sentence to improve a sentence-length statistic.
- Do not add a question because the venue often uses questions.
- Do not add a parenthetical because Matthew uses parentheticals.
- Do not turn a warm sentence clinical because it violates a rhythm target.
- Do not manufacture specificity to make prose more vivid.

**Metrics diagnose writing. They do not generate it.**

## 2. Two models, never averaged

### The author model

Source: Matthew's unedited conversational writing, not his published posts. The posts are a
contaminated sample.

> "Sometimes that's ok! Marketers (and leaders, in general) sometimes need to know someone else is
> going through it. This doesn't always have to be thought leadership - sometimes it just to relate
> to people. I don't want to ALWAYS be in thought leadership and data moves, proving points. That's
> not my vibe."

> "While I like certain part of the new post, it's fundamentally a different topic than the original.
> Also, there are some weird paragraph disconnections. It's also too abstract - it's almost like it's
> missing human connection flow."

> "There's no mic drop because the thoughts are joined."

Take from this: **diction, stance, degree of certainty, relationship with the reader, contraction
habit, willingness to interrupt himself, preference for plain judgment, and what kinds of claims
sound natural coming from him.**

Do not take sentence architecture. Speech runs in short bursts; prose that reads well does not.
Copying the architecture of speech produces the staccato that reads as generated.

### The venue model

Source: Inside Higher Ed's Call to Action corpus, 15 measured bodies.

Take from this only: **article scale, expected development, evidence practices, degree of
argumentation, reader assumptions, publication conventions.**

### The rule

**Never average the two into a generic higher education thought-leadership voice.** Matthew should
sound like Matthew writing an Inside Higher Ed article, not like the median contributor.

## 3. Non-negotiables

Two things are absolute. Everything else in this document is preference or diagnostic.

### Provenance

Every assertion about Matthew's life, experience, behavior, emotions, surroundings, history or
actions must trace to information he actually supplied. That includes names, numbers, quantities,
durations, places, sensory details, emotions, habits, conversations, past actions, accomplishments,
mistakes and magnitudes.

Never infer autobiographical experience because it would strengthen the paragraph.

**Never upgrade participation into leadership, observation into action, or familiarity into personal
experience.** If he says he attended a session, do not write that he ran one. If he says something
frustrated him, do not invent the setting or his reaction.

When a useful detail is missing there are three legal choices: ask for it, mark it clearly as a
placeholder, or write the precise but unembellished version.

**Precision is not color.** "The meeting was about enrollment strategy" is permissible if supplied.
"The stale coffee and fluorescent conference room made the discussion feel even longer" is not
permissible unless supplied.

### No em dashes or en dashes

Author preference, absolute. Period, comma, colon or parentheses instead. One standing exception: a
coined term such as `Proof&ndash;Pride&ndash;Promise` is a name, not prose.

## 4. Write from a semantic spine

Before drafting prose, determine what the piece is actually doing.

Build the spine as a list of the major thought moves. Each move answers:

1. What does the reader understand here that they did not understand before?
2. Why does this thought belong immediately after the previous one?
3. What does it make possible for the next part?

Paragraphs do not need to perform exactly one function. They do need to participate in a continuous
line of thought.

**The adjacency test.** For every adjacent pair of paragraphs, complete this sentence internally:

> "Because the previous paragraph established ______, this paragraph can now ______."

If that relationship cannot be stated clearly, inspect the transition or the structure. **Do not hide
the gap with connective language.** Avoid transitions that simulate continuity when the underlying
thoughts are not connected: "But here's the thing," "That's where this gets interesting," "And that
matters because."

**Matthew's governing principle:** *"There's no mic drop because the thoughts are joined."* A landing
works because the piece has arrived somewhere, not because the final sentence was engineered to sound
final.

## 5. Post types as reader contracts

These define the writer's relationship with the reader. They are not structural templates.

### Argument

Matthew believes something the reader may reasonably question, resist, or not yet have considered.

Establish the problem, develop the reasoning, use evidence where the argument depends on external
facts, and commit to a position by the end.

**The ending does not require a literal directive.** It may recommend action, state an implication,
name a choice, sharpen the argument, or say what he believes should happen.

### Solidarity

The primary purpose is recognition. The reader should feel that someone who understands the work has
named an experience accurately.

No research or data required unless it naturally belongs.

**Do not force it to become thought leadership.** No manufactured lesson, framework, numbered
takeaway or professional-development conclusion added to justify publishing.

May end with recognition, uncertainty, modest perspective, companionship, or an unresolved thought.

### Field note

Matthew noticed something worth examining. Enough context for the observation to matter, without
becoming an argument. May end inconclusively.

**Inconclusive does not mean shapeless.** By the end the reader should understand what he noticed and
why it stayed with him.

---

## 6. Stance and human connection

Matthew is not always teaching. **Do not automatically position him above the reader.**

He may be thinking beside the reader, admitting uncertainty, describing something difficult, noticing
something small, changing his mind, or saying in effect "this happens to me too."

- Avoid turning every observation into expertise.
- Avoid extracting a leadership lesson from every personal experience.
- Avoid summarizing the emotional meaning of a passage the passage already communicates.
- Allow some sentences to exist because they are true and recognizable, not because they advance a
  thesis.

**Warmth is not a metric violation.**

### Self-interruption

Parentheticals, qualification and self-interruption are part of his natural stance, but they must
arise from thought.

An aside may qualify the claim, reveal uncertainty, admit a limitation, acknowledge another
interpretation, or briefly show him thinking. **It may never introduce a new autobiographical fact
that has not been supplied.**

Do not insert asides to make prose appear human. **Do not systematically make him self-deprecating**;
that becomes its own synthetic tic.

The test: does the aside change the reader's understanding of his stance toward the surrounding
sentence? If not, remove it.

---

## 7. Specificity without fabrication

When prose becomes abstract, **identify the noun that has lost its referent.** Do not replace vagueness
with invented texture.

Prefer named concepts over placeholder abstractions:

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| "the thing actually deciding" | what is actually doing the deciding, if known |
| "the gap" | which gap |
| "the statement" | which statement |

Track demonstratives and vague referents (this, that, it, thing, issue, gap, process, approach,
conversation) when their antecedents are unclear. **A reader should not have to reverse engineer what
a noun refers to.**

---

## 8. Endings from entailment

Do not write the ending separately as a punchline. It must be earned by the article.

Before accepting a final paragraph, ask:

1. What has the piece actually established?
2. What can Matthew now say that he could not responsibly have said in paragraph two?
3. Does the closing sentence refer clearly to something already developed?
4. Would this line still sound plausible pasted onto ten unrelated higher education leadership posts?
   If so it is too generic.

Solidarity and field notes may end unresolved. Arguments should commit, without performing.

**Never invent an aphorism because the article needs a strong close.**

---

## 9. Rhetorical patterns, treated structurally

There is no banned-phrase dictionary here, because lexical avoidance does not solve the problem.

Watch for these repeated rhetorical moves:

- False contrast, a thought manufactured into "not X but Y"
- Rule-of-three phrasing used for cadence rather than meaning
- Cleft constructions creating artificial emphasis
- Short fragments written solely for drama
- Generic reversals
- Rhetorical questions whose answers were just given
- Questions inserted to meet a quota
- Universal statements shaped as "The institutions that X will Y"
- Aphoristic closing lines that could be pasted onto an unrelated leadership article
- Escalating sequences of abstract nouns
- Repeated paragraphs ending in mini conclusions

**Any single instance may be legitimate. Recurrence is the signal.** When a device appears, ask
whether the idea naturally requires that construction. If the same move recurs, rewrite the weaker
instances.

**Do not solve a flagged pattern by changing punctuation or swapping words.** Rewriting a
two-sentence tic as a one-sentence tic with a semicolon hides it from the audit and from the writer,
and not from the reader.

## 10. Diagnostics

Two audits, in this order. The semantic one comes first because it catches what counting cannot.

### The semantic audit

Run before the script. See `drafts/semantic-audit.md` for the worksheet.

**Referent audit.** For every ambiguous this, that, it, they, thing, gap, statement, process,
approach, identify the antecedent. If it is not obvious on a normal read, rewrite.

**Paragraph adjacency audit.** For each paragraph after the first, explain why it follows the
previous one. If the explanation depends only on a transition phrase rather than an idea
relationship, repair the structure.

**Claim support audit.** Identify the strongest claims. For each, determine whether it rests on
supplied experience, external evidence, reasoning developed in the piece, or clearly framed personal
judgment. **Do not let confident syntax substitute for support.**

**Metaphor audit.** Identify metaphors carrying argumentative weight. Check whether each still works
when extended through the sentence or paragraph. Do not mix metaphor systems for variety.

**Ending entailment audit.** Identify exactly which earlier material makes the final claim possible.
If the ending asserts more than the body established, develop the reasoning or reduce the claim.

**Genericity audit.** Find sentences that could appear unchanged in an unrelated article about
leadership, innovation, branding, culture or strategy. Generic is not automatically bad; a
concentration of interchangeable sentences means the author's actual thought has disappeared.

### The measurement script

```bash
python3 scripts/voice-audit.py <file>              # gates, candidates, reference values
python3 scripts/voice-audit.py --claims <file>     # provenance review list
python3 scripts/voice-audit.py --worksheet <file>  # semantic audit scaffolding
```

Three tiers, and the distinction matters:

**Hard gates.** Only things that are genuinely binary. Dashes. Unfilled placeholders in a draft
marked final. That is the whole list.

**Review candidates.** Instances listed with context for human judgment, with recurrence noted.
Repeated architecture within one article matters more than a rate per thousand words.

**Reference values.** Descriptive only. Word count, sentence-length distribution, paragraph length.
For an Inside Higher Ed argument, roughly 700 to 1,100 words is a useful reference range, not a pass
or fail.

**No quotas.** Questions, sentences beginning with "The", contractions and semicolons are descriptive
signals, not quality targets. Fifteen articles establish rough venue character and are too few to
establish precise norms for rare constructions.

The script never rewrites prose to satisfy a metric.

**After any revision prompted by a metric, reread the whole paragraph for warmth, meaning and
continuity. If the prose got less human while the number improved, restore the better prose.**

## 11. The workflow

1. **Interview** when the piece depends on experience. Collect the anchor material first. The anchor
   may be an experience, observation, tension, decision, question, conversation, fact, contradiction,
   or something he cannot stop thinking about. It does not have to be a story.
   Ask for concrete facts before interpretation: what happened, who was involved, what he expected,
   what surprised him, what he got wrong, what changed, and any wording he remembers using.
   **Capture his phrasing.** It carries stance and diction.
2. **Name the reader relationship** in one sentence: "I am trying to convince the reader that…" / "I
   am trying to name an experience the reader may recognize…" / "I noticed something and I want to
   think about why it stayed with me…"
3. **Build the semantic spine.**
4. **Draft**, without optimizing against statistics.
5. **Semantic audit.**
6. **Provenance audit.**
7. **Rhetorical pattern audit.**
8. **Only then, the measurement script.**

Metrics may tell you where to reread. They may not tell you how to rewrite.

---

## 12. Usable out of the gate

The goal is not a rough draft Matthew has to humanize. A first complete draft should already have:

- a coherent line of thought
- clear paragraph-to-paragraph movement
- no invented biography or texture
- natural variation in sentence shape
- few obvious generative rhetorical patterns
- an ending earned by the body
- the appropriate relationship with the reader
- enough of his diction and stance to sound recognizably like the same person

**Do not knowingly leave AI-shaped prose for a later humanization pass.** A humanization pass refines
credible writing; it does not rescue generated copy.
