I've come to think of this as being *opportunity rich*.

It's an exciting position to be in. You see potential everywhere. There are processes that could be improved, technologies that could be introduced, relationships that could be strengthened, and ideas that have been waiting for someone to give them a little attention.

And if you're anything like me, your mind immediately starts connecting the dots. You can see how one improvement might enable another, how a relatively small investment could solve a longstanding problem, or how something the organization has been doing for years could be approached differently.

Before long, you've mentally developed about three years of work. You've been on the job for two weeks.

The temptation is to start doing everything. After all, you were hired to lead, bring fresh thinking, and make things better. You want to demonstrate progress, support your team, and show the organization what's possible.

But there's a difference between recognizing opportunity and having the organizational capacity to pursue it.

And learning to manage that difference might be one of the most important things a new leader can do.

## First, Understand What You're Actually Trying to Accomplish

When stepping into a new leadership role, I think there's tremendous value in resisting the urge to immediately develop a long list of initiatives.

That's easier said than done, particularly when people are eager to share what needs fixing. And they will share. Sometimes enthusiastically.

But before deciding what to do, we need to understand what the organization is trying to accomplish.

Most institutions already have some form of strategic plan, even if it's sitting on a shelf somewhere collecting dust. Ideally, it's a living document that guides institutional decisions and investments. Either way, it's a place to begin.

What are the organization's broad priorities? Where does leadership want to see meaningful progress? What commitments have already been made to the people the organization serves?

From there, I like the idea of developing a relatively small set of broad objectives for the division or function I'm leading. These should directly support the organization's goals, while providing enough flexibility to accommodate opportunities that emerge along the way.

For a marketing organization, those objectives might involve strengthening institutional reputation, improving audience engagement, building more sophisticated marketing operations, or developing a more coordinated approach to brand management.

The objectives provide direction without prematurely prescribing every project.

This connection to institutional strategy matters. In my doctoral research examining public university presidents' perceptions of marketing effectiveness, strategic alignment emerged as an important theme in how presidents understood marketing's contribution to their institutions.

My research also found an association between having the chief marketing officer serve on the university cabinet and higher perceived marketing effectiveness among the presidents in the sample. Interestingly, a direct reporting relationship to the president wasn't significantly associated with perceived effectiveness.

Those findings don't establish cause and effect, but they reinforce something I've come to appreciate throughout my career. Where a function sits on an organizational chart doesn't tell us nearly as much as we'd like about whether it's meaningfully contributing to the institution's direction.

And if we're going to prioritize opportunities, we'd better understand that direction first.

## Figure Out Where You Are Before Deciding Where You're Going

One of the more useful approaches I've found is developing a maturity model.

I know. Another model. Just what every organization needs.

But hear me out.

A maturity model doesn't have to be a complicated consulting exercise involving a hundred-page report and a color-coded spreadsheet that requires its own instruction manual.

At its simplest, it's a structured way to evaluate where an organization stands across the capabilities that matter to its success.

For a marketing operation, those capabilities might include market research, brand management, digital infrastructure, marketing technology, analytics, creative operations, governance, and collaboration with institutional partners.

The idea is to establish a baseline. How developed are these capabilities today? Where are we operating effectively? Where are we relying on manual processes, individual expertise, or systems that haven't kept up with the organization's needs?

And importantly, what does a more mature version of each capability actually look like?

That last question is worth spending time on because maturity isn't necessarily about having the newest technology, the largest team, or the most sophisticated processes.

It's about having the capabilities appropriate to what the organization is trying to accomplish.

A small, highly effective team with well-defined processes might be more mature in certain areas than a much larger operation with expensive technology nobody knows how to use.

The baseline also needs to be developed with the people doing the work. A new leader can bring a valuable outside perspective, but there's a good chance the team knows precisely where the problems are. They may have been trying to address them for years.

Understanding that history can save everyone considerable frustration.

## Give Yourself a Year. Then Three. Then Five.

Once there's a reasonable understanding of where things stand, I like looking at development across one-, three-, and five-year horizons.

Not because we can predict what the organization will need five years from now. We can't. But because some capabilities take considerably longer to build than others.

In the first year, the emphasis might be on establishing foundational processes, strengthening relationships, addressing immediate operational barriers, and creating a more consistent approach to measurement.

By year three, the organization might be integrating systems, developing more sophisticated audience insights, or coordinating work across departments in ways that weren't previously possible.

By year five, the aspiration could be an operation capable of more advanced predictive analysis, continuous optimization, and contributing more consistently to institutional strategy.

Those aren't universal stages, of course. Different organizations will have different needs, resources, and starting points.

The point is to create a reasonable picture of what progress could look like over time.

And I think there's something psychologically helpful about this approach, too.

When you're surrounded by opportunity, everything can feel urgent. A maturity plan gives you permission to recognize that something is important without pretending it must be accomplished immediately.

Some things belong in year one. Others need to wait until the foundational work is complete.

That's not a lack of ambition. It's sequencing.

## Not Everything Worth Improving Has a Convenient KPI

I'm a big believer in measurement. If we're investing institutional resources, we should be able to explain what we're trying to accomplish and whether we're making progress.

But I've also seen how easily organizations can become overly focused on what they can count.

Campaign performance, applications, revenue, conversion rates, and awareness are all important measures. They tell us something about outcomes.

But what about the work required to make those outcomes possible?

How do you measure progress in building trust between departments? Establishing a functional governance structure? Improving the quality of strategic conversations? Developing a team that's more capable of handling increasingly complex work?

You can certainly identify indicators for these things, but assigning an arbitrary percentage improvement doesn't necessarily make the measurement meaningful.

This is another reason I find maturity models useful.

Instead of pretending every improvement can be expressed as a conventional KPI, we can establish observable characteristics of progress.

Maybe today, project requests arrive through emails, hallway conversations, and the occasional panicked phone call. Six months from now, there's a shared intake process. A year from now, the organization has agreed on prioritization criteria, established project visibility, and can make more informed decisions about capacity.

We can observe and document that progression.

And while those improvements might eventually contribute to faster delivery, better resource allocation, or stronger campaign results, they have value before every downstream outcome can be measured.

Of course, a maturity model shouldn't become an excuse to avoid accountability. If we're claiming progress, we should be able to point to evidence that something has actually changed.

Sometimes that's a number. Sometimes it's a demonstrated capability, an adopted process, or a decision the organization can now make with greater confidence.

The important thing is knowing the difference.

## You Still Have to Decide What Comes First

A maturity plan can help organize opportunities, but it doesn't make the difficult decisions for you.

There will still be more good ideas than available time, money, or people.

Some opportunities will have an immediate connection to institutional priorities. Others will address a significant operational risk. Some will create the foundation for several future improvements.

And occasionally, something will simply need to wait because the team is already carrying more than it reasonably should.

I think new leaders need to be particularly mindful of that last point.

When we arrive with fresh energy and a head full of ideas, it's easy to forget that our teams have been doing the work long before we got there. They have existing responsibilities, commitments, and institutional knowledge that deserves consideration.

What looks like an exciting new initiative to a leader might look like another thirty hours of work to someone who's already stretched thin.

That's why prioritization needs to be a conversation, not simply a declaration.

I also think it's important to identify a few opportunities for early progress. Not manufactured quick wins designed to make a new leader look productive, but meaningful improvements that address genuine needs and demonstrate the value of a more deliberate approach.

Sometimes those early successes create the confidence and organizational support needed to pursue more difficult work later.

And sometimes the best early decision is to stop doing something that no longer serves the organization particularly well.

We don't always talk enough about that option.

## Remember That the Plan Is Supposed to Help You Lead

There's a risk in all of this, of course. We can spend so much time developing objectives, assessing maturity, and building roadmaps that planning becomes another thing competing for the organization's attention.

I've seen enough strategic planning exercises to know that a beautifully formatted document isn't necessarily evidence of strategic thinking.

The plan needs to be useful.

It should help leaders explain priorities, make resource decisions, establish reasonable expectations, and have more productive conversations with their teams and institutional partners.

It should also change when circumstances change. A five-year maturity plan isn't a five-year contract with yourself.

New technology will emerge. Institutional priorities will evolve. Budgets will change. Some opportunities will prove less valuable than expected, while others will become far more important.

That's fine. The purpose is to give the organization a direction and a way to evaluate progress, not to predict the future with remarkable precision.

Being opportunity rich is a good problem to have. It means there's potential, room to grow, and meaningful work ahead.

But opportunity without prioritization can quickly become exhaustion, even for the most enthusiastic leader and capable team.

You don't have to accomplish everything in your first year. You probably shouldn't try.

The goal is to make thoughtful decisions about what matters, build the capacity to pursue it, and create a way to recognize progress along the way.

And perhaps, occasionally, enjoy the satisfaction of finishing something before adding another twelve ideas to the list.
